Folio Inquiry

Photo: Travis Leery / Unsplash

Theme

Experience & Exploration.

Concept

Keeper's Watch places the user inside an abandoned coastal lighthouse and its immediate grounds at dusk — the lamp room, the cliff path, and the rocky shoreline below. There is no combat or objective; the experience is built entirely around quiet spatial discovery, environmental storytelling (what happened to the keeper?), and atmosphere carried by sound.

Target XR Platform

Meta Quest 3S (standalone, room-scale VR) — the headset I actually own. It runs the same Snapdragon XR2 Gen 2 chipset and 8GB RAM as the Quest 3 (Road to VR, 2024), so the performance headroom I'm designing the models around (see Asset Plan) applies here too. The 3S mainly differs in display resolution (1832×1920 vs 2064×2208) and field of view (96° vs 110°) — that changes how sharp it looks, not what I can actually model.

I picked it because it's untethered and has good hand/controller tracking for close-up interaction with small props — rotating a lobster trap, picking up a lantern. The tight performance budget is also useful pressure: it keeps me optimising instead of just chasing detail.

Target Audience

Adults (16+) interested in atmospheric, narrative-driven exploration experiences — the "walking simulator" VR audience (players of Firewatch, Dear Esther, What Remains of Edith Finch) — plus a secondary audience of local heritage/maritime history groups who could use a piece like this for informal digital-preservation or tourism storytelling.

Ethical Considerations

  • Cultural/historical respect: the keeper and their story are made up. I didn't want to put words in a real family's mouth just to make the concept sound more grounded.
  • Comfort/accessibility: VR makes people sick easily, so I'm using teleport locomotion instead of free movement, and the cliff edges get a soft boundary for anyone with vertigo.
  • Content suitability: no jump-scares, despite the abandoned setting. This needs to stay a quiet, atmospheric piece, not tip into horror territory.

Why This Concept Fits the Audience

A quiet, abandoned space is a hard thing to sell on a flat screen — it needs presence to land. Standing at the actual scale of the lamp room, hearing the foghorn come from a specific direction outside, noticing how small the space feels compared to the tower's height from outside — that's the kind of thing VR does that video or photos of the same lighthouse can't. For walking-sim VR players, that's the entire appeal of the genre; for heritage groups, it's a way to let people feel a place's atmosphere even after the real site has been automated or fallen into disrepair.

For the heritage/maritime audience specifically, there's a social thread behind the emptiness that's the point of the piece, not just its backdrop: real lighthouses have been automated for decades — a light doesn't need a person standing next to it to switch on. But a keeper watching the water sees things a sensor doesn't: a boat that "looks wrong" before anything's technically an emergency, a change in the weather a machine wouldn't flag until it's too late. Automation removed the job, but not the reason someone was needed to do it. Keeper's Watch isn't just nostalgia for an old building — the empty lamp room and the keeper's absence are meant to make that loss actually felt, not just stated.
