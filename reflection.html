Critical Self-Evaluation

Photo: Evgeni Tcherkasski / Unsplash

1. Shift in Perspective

Three weeks into this unit, the biggest change is that I now think about performance limits from the start instead of treating them as a problem for later. Before this, my instinct with any creative project was to build first and worry about constraints after. Planning Keeper's Watch against the Quest 3S's actual triangle and draw-call budget (Meta, n.d.) forced me to design each prop's level of detail before I've modelled anything, which is a different way of working for me.

2. Scoping & Prioritisation

I already simplified the Lobster Trap and Pier Post from my original plan. I'd initially pictured the trap with individually modelled woven rope and the pier posts each showing unique weathering and damage. Realistically, as someone who hasn't done much box modelling yet, that level of per-object detail wasn't a good use of time this early — it's a background/detail prop, not the hero asset. I simplified both to rely more on baked texture detail (rope weave, wood grain, weathering) rather than modelled geometry, which keeps them under budget and achievable without slowing down the Lighthouse Lamp Housing, which is where the real modelling effort should go.

3. Sourcing My Technical Guides

I don't have anything bookmarked yet, so I went looking for reputable channels instead of just grabbing the first search result:

  • Blender Guru (Andrew Price) — youtube.com/@blenderguru — widely recommended beginner box-modelling series (his "donut" tutorial is the standard starting point most Blender learners are pointed to).
  • Audacity's own editing documentation — support.audacityteam.org/basics/audacity-editing — for the core editing workflow (import, trim, layer) before touching looping or noise cleanup on the raw sound files.

4. Identifying Skill Gaps

3D modelling basics is what I'm least confident about — I haven't really used Blender before this unit. My plan is to work through the Blender Guru beginner series on a throwaway practice object (not one of my actual folio props) before Week 5, so I've made my early mistakes somewhere that doesn't cost me time on the Lighthouse Lamp Housing itself. If box modelling turns out to take longer than expected, that's exactly why the Lobster Trap and Pier Post are already scoped down — that's my Plan B built in from the start.

5. Progressive Folio 2 Update

The Lighthouse Lamp Housing’s gallery railing was the most challenging part to construct. Although it is a relatively simple design, consisting of a ring of cylinders, getting the proportions right was a matter of trial and error. Creating a set of posts and a rail that would be the correct size on the deck was not as simple to execute as the sketch suggested. The deck was considerably simpler, as it was a simple cylinder serving as a floor. The window, door, and steps were similarly simple to make, as boxes.

The most challenging part of the Lobster Trap to make was the wire mesh frame. It required an entirely different approach than simply scaling a block up or down. Furthermore, creating the weave that formed the funnel’s entrance proved particularly challenging.

6. Final Folio Update

Scope rather than specifications. In contrast to the Pier's initial design, which called for a single repeating post, I chose to build the whole branching boardwalk instead. The rationale goes beyond aesthetic appeal: instead of just meeting the criteria, I wanted my folio to show how my skills have improved over the course of the semester. I made the deliberate choice to finish the complete structure in order to push myself and show that I could handle more; finishing the most basic version would only have shown that I could follow a specification.

The export "bug" wasn't really a bug. When the Lobster Trap first exported to SketchFab completely white, I initially treated it as a Blender/glTF limitation to work around. Looking back, the real cause was that I hadn't set the correct glTF export settings (Materials: Export, Apply Modifiers ticked) — once I understood that, it wasn't a mysterious failure, it was a process step I'd been skipping. That's also why I had to bake the procedural wood/paint materials to actual image files before export — glTF can't read Blender's shader node graphs directly, only flat values or real images, so baking became a necessary step, not an optional polish pass.

Changing tools while working on the job. After experiencing problems with DaVinci Resolve for audio editing, I moved to Audacity, which I found to be more effective.

What I'd do differently: the Seagull Nest. This is the fifth stretch asset that I cut. In retrospect, it is far more difficult to model anything organic — a living creature rather than a manufactured object — than the hard-surface props I actually produced (posts, boats, and traps all have flat panels and predictable forms; a nest doesn't). With additional time, I believe I could have completed it, but this close to the deadline, the trade-off of allocating that time to audio and Scene Integration was the right decision.

Compared to Folio 2. My last submission was criticised for not being critical enough and for focusing too much on future goals rather than actual evaluation. This time, instead of just outlining my intentions, I focused more on really adhering to the rubric criteria and making sure each part addressed what was actually being judged.
